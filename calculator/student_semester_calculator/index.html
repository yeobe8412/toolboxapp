Student Semester Calculator

The Student Semester Calculator summarizes the academic load and estimated performance for one semester. It combines course credits, expected grades, and weekly study hours to produce a credit-weighted semester average and a practical workload view. Students can use it while selecting classes or revising a schedule before registration closes.

The calculation treats the entered grade as a percentage and weights it by course credits. Weekly study hours are shown alongside the credit total so an academically strong schedule can still be checked for time pressure. Institutional GPA conversion rules vary, so the percentage result should be compared with the school’s own grading scale.

Semester average = Σ(Course credits × Course grade) ÷ Σ(Course credits)

The calculation uses percentage grades and credit weighting. Study hours per credit equals weekly study hours divided by total credits. It does not convert percentages to an institution-specific GPA.

Review the stated assumptions before using the estimate for a decision.

Given: 3 credits at 85%, 3 credits at 90%, 4 credits at 78%, and 18 weekly study hours.

Calculation: (3×85 + 3×90 + 4×78) ÷ 10 = 837 ÷ 10 = 83.7%. Study hours per credit = 18 ÷ 10 = 1.8.

Result: The estimated semester average is 83.7%.

Why are grades weighted by credits?

A higher-credit course usually represents more instructional work and therefore contributes more to a semester average.

Can I use letter grades?

Convert each letter grade to the percentage or point value required by your institution before entering it.

What if I take more than three courses?

Combine similar courses into one weighted entry or calculate groups separately. A full institutional record may require a dedicated GPA calculator.

Does study time affect the grade result?

No. Study time is reported as a workload metric and does not mathematically change the expected grade.

Can this predict my official semester GPA?

Not necessarily. Schools use different grade-point scales, repeated-course rules, and rounding policies.
